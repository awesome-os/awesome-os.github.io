Chapter 53: Wide-Open Doors and the Easy Money

When you spend years building high-speed ticket parsers and keeping multi-million-visitor streaming platforms alive under brutal traffic spikes, you develop a strange, relentless kind of X-ray vision. You stop seeing shiny websites and corporate logos; instead, you see the exposed plumbing underneath. You notice the unguarded endpoints, the sloppy caching layers burning fortunes in wasted bandwidth, the fragile billing pipelines held together by luck and duct tape.

In the quiet weeks after his heartbreak, when Frank's pockets were thin and his pride was bruised, those wide-open flaws seemed to glow in the dark. Opportunities for easy money were everywhere. One client after another came knocking with the same unspoken pitch: Don't fix the foundation, Frank. Just wrap the mess in something fast and shiny so we can pitch investors by Friday. We'll pay double. Others hinted at gray-area scraping tricks and bloated enterprise contracts where you could bill for six months of theater while delivering a hollow shell.

One evening, a smooth-talking broker slid a contract across a café table between two espressos. The numbers on the page were dizzying—enough to clear every worry, buy the fastest workstation on the market, and silence every voice that wondered why Frank worked so hard for so little applause. All he had to do was bolt a flashy dashboard onto a broken data pipeline, sign off on performance metrics he knew were cooked with cherry-picked benchmarks, and smile for the launch call.

Frank stared at the signature line while the café's espresso machine hissed behind him. He could see every flaw in their stack without even opening a laptop. He could have exploited their blind spots, taken the check, and walked away rich before the system collapsed six months later under real users. Nobody in that boardroom would have known the difference—except the machines, and except Frank.

He pictured his father's tired hands all those years ago, working long, honest hours just to bring home a single glowing Atari. His father had never built a life on tricks or fake numbers. Slowly, Frank closed the folder, slid the pen back beside the saucer, and looked the broker straight in the eye. There were wide-open doors to easy money all around him, and every single one of them led into a smaller, uglier version of himself.

Frank's Memory Note

Seeing every flaw in the world is a test of character: you can exploit the cracks for fast cash, or you can use your eyes to build something worthy.
