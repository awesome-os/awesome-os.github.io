Chapter 22: Cassette Decks and Clock Cycles

If the computer was the place where Frank learned how to think, music was the place where he learned how to breathe. His desk soon gained a second altar right beside the keyboard: a scuffed stereo deck with twin VU meters whose needles twitched like nervous birds whenever a bassline dropped. Tapes stacked in crooked towers—dubbed mixes from friends, hand-labeled in cramped ballpoint ink, alongside worn-out albums whose plastic cases had hairline cracks across the corners. When he slipped his oversized foam headphones over his ears and hit play, the hiss of magnetic tape felt like stepping through a velvet curtain.

At first, he thought music was simply a soundtrack to keep his hands moving while he typed. Then one rainy Tuesday, while listening to a driving synth-heavy track on repeat, he noticed something that made him stop mid-line. The song was built like a piece of architecture. A steady kick drum anchored the clock signal; a repeating arpeggio looped cleanly over four bars; a warm pad floated like shared memory underneath; and right when your ear expected the pattern to stay safe, a snare fill broke the symmetry and lifted the entire room two inches off the floor.

Music, Frank realized, was mathematics that had learned how to ache. You could measure every frequency in hertz and plot every waveform on an oscilloscope, yet the exact same chord progression could make you want to sprint down an empty street or sit completely still with a lump in your throat. No datasheet explained why a minor seventh resolved into a major triad with a feeling like coming home after a long winter. You didn't compile a song; you surrendered to it and let it rewire your pulse from the inside out.

He started trading mixtapes the way other kids traded rumors. Putting together a sixty-minute cassette for someone required a surprising amount of precision: calculating track lengths so a song wouldn't get brutally chopped off by the end of Side A, matching tempos so the transition from one track to the next felt inevitable, and slipping in one brave, slightly vulnerable song near the middle that said the things you weren't quite bold enough to speak out loud. Sitting cross-legged on the carpet with a pencil ready to wind slack ribbon back into a spool, he felt like an audio engineer building tiny emotional time capsules.

Even his coding changed under the influence of rhythm. He stopped writing cramped, breathless walls of instructions and began hearing the cadence of a function—the setup, the tension of a branch, the clean release of a return. When a build finally clicked into place right as a guitar solo peaked in his headphones, the grin that broke across his face had nothing to do with productivity. It was pure joy, loud and unashamed.

Years later, he would still remember the exact smell of warm amplifier dust and the way green equalizer bars danced in the dark of his bedroom. Long before he knew how to name half the feelings rising inside him, a four-minute track through worn-out speakers already knew every word.

Frank's Memory Note

Music is proof that structure and emotion were never opposites—they are the same frequency heard through different ears.
