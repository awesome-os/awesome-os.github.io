Chapter 23: The Roof Under the Satellites

You can build a lot of things alone in a bedroom, Frank learned, but you cannot build a life there. What saved him from turning into a ghost behind glass was a small, fiercely loyal circle of friends who didn't care that his mind ran three steps ahead of most conversations—in fact, they loved him for it. They were a mismatched crew: a quiet artist who sketched impossible cities on the backs of receipts, a loud-laughing drummer who could turn any park bench into a percussion kit, and a sharp-tongued night owl who argued about philosophy with the same intensity Frank reserved for kernel loops.

Their favorite meeting place wasn't a mall or a noisy arcade; it was the flat gravel roof of an old garage behind the drummer's house, reached by a rusted iron ladder and a silent agreement not to tell anybody's parents. On warm Friday nights they climbed up with cheap sodas, a battered portable stereo, and jackets folded into makeshift pillows. Lying flat on their backs on the still-warm tar paper, they watched the sky turn from burnt orange to deep velvet while the neighborhood's streetlights flickered awake one by one.

Up there, nobody had to pretend to have it all figured out. They passed a single pair of earbuds back and forth, each one taking a side so the stereo image split between two heads, and talked until their voices went hoarse. They argued about whether time really flowed in one direction, whether machines could ever dream, why grown-ups seemed so tired all the time, and what they would build if someone handed them a blank map and told them to start over. Frank would explain a wild idea about distributed networks or virtual worlds, gesturing wildly at the constellations, and instead of rolling their eyes, his friends would lean in and ask, "Okay, and what happens next?"

Sometimes the best part wasn't even the talking. It was the twenty-minute stretches of comfortable silence where the only sound was a distant train horn and the faint tinny bleed of music from a speaker resting on the gravel. A satellite would drift silently across the Big Dipper—a tiny, steady point of reflected sunlight moving too smoothly to be a plane—and someone would point without saying a word until all four of them were tracking it together.

In those friendships, Frank discovered a kind of bandwidth no copper wire could ever carry. It was the relief of being completely understood without having to compress yourself first. When one of them had a terrible week at home or bombed a test or felt invisible at school, the roof was there, and so were the others, ready with bad jokes, warm fries split four ways, and the quiet certainty that nobody had to walk home in the dark alone.

Climbing back down the ladder past midnight, his palms smelling of iron and cool night dew, Frank always felt anchored. The world was huge and chaotic, sure—but he had a crew, and they knew his real voice.

Frank's Memory Note

The right friends don't ask you to shrink your signal—they climb onto the roof and help you aim it at the sky.
