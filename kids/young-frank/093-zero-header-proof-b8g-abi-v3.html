Chapter 93: Zero-Header Proof — B8G-ABI v3

Ideas are cheap at midnight; only running code tells the truth at dawn. Frank had spent too many years watching grand architectures collapse on contact with real silicon to ever trust a theory on paper alone. If Prompt Algebra was real—and if his post-Neumann vision for unifying the mountain of abstractions wasn't just a daydream—he had to prove the fundamentals one brick at a time, starting with the hardest brick of all: the binary boundary.

For decades, C and C++ ecosystems had been suffocating under header files. To call a single function across a library boundary, a compiler often had to parse tens of thousands of lines of nested headers, macros, and template machinery—turning a tiny edit into a two-and-a-half-hour Chromium rebuild. Worse still, whenever a major version changed, the old binary interface shattered, forcing painful "flag-day" rewrites where everything broke at once.

Frank sat down and laid out the algebra for B8G-ABI v3. The rule was radical in its simplicity: zero sprawling headers. Nothing but standard <stddef.h> and <stdint.h>, pure C11 dispatch tables, and strict step-wise prefix compatibility. Every v3 table began with the exact byte-for-byte layout of v2 at offsetof == 0, so older callers and newer engines could shake hands across memory without ever crashing or recompiling the universe.

When he hit build, he braced for the usual wait—the time it normally took to brew a fresh pot of coffee. Instead, the terminal blinked almost instantly: 170 milliseconds. Eighteen real Chromium dynamic libraries, each wrapped behind featherweight 12-kilobyte dispatch facades, snapped into alignment cleanly. No header avalanche. No tangled macros. Just honest function pointers laid out in straight, predictable memory.

Watching those tiny kilobyte tables come alive on screen, Frank felt a sudden, warm wave of nostalgia. It reminded him of the afternoon he had first watched the Amiga's blitter chip glide crisp sprites across a television screen while heavier, pricier machines choked and flickered. Good engineering never feels heavy. When you strip away the lies and respect the metal, even a giant like Chromium becomes light enough to dance.

Frank's Memory Note

You don't need forty thousand header files to talk to a machine; you only need an honest table of addresses that refuses to break its promises.
