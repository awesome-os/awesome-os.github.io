Chapter 71: Trusting the Source Code Over the Pitch

The pitches came in glossy slide decks with pastel gradients and words like disruptive and next-generation stretched across forty-point fonts. Every few months, someone in a chat room, a conference hallway, or a late-night call would lean in and tell Frank that V8 was yesterday's news, that Chromium was too heavy, that some brand-new runtime or boutique engine was about to sweep the board clean. They always had benchmarks printed on neat bar charts. They almost never had scars.

Frank had stopped believing bar charts back when he was tuning servers for millions of impatient visitors who didn't care whose logo sat on the press release. When someone pitched him a miracle engine, he didn't argue in the comments. He cloned the repository, poured himself a mug of black coffee, and opened the source tree in the quiet blue glow of his monitor.

What he found in the alternative pitches almost always told the same story: clever prototypes held together by optimistic assumptions, thin edge-case handling, and a mountain of unexamined dependencies waiting to buckle the moment real-world traffic hit them. Then he opened V8 and Chromium. They were huge, yes—sprawling cities of C++ and build graphs that made newcomers flinch—but when Frank walked their streets, he saw load-bearing stone. He saw decades of battle-tested security mitigations, hardened parsers, memory fences written by engineers who had survived real exploits, and commit histories that read like field journals from the front lines of computing.

It reminded him of the old days cracking open a beige PC tower or peering into an Amiga's schematics. Marketing could promise you the moon, but copper traces didn't lie, and neither did source code. If you read the hot paths—the object layouts, the garbage collector barriers, the sandbox boundaries—you could hear the honesty of the people who built them.

Choosing V8 and Chromium wasn't about following the crowd or worshipping a giant tech company. In fact, plenty of his peers thought he was being stubborn, even old-fashioned, for refusing to chase the newest shiny framework. But Frank wasn't chasing trends. He was laying a foundation meant to hold an entire unified computing mountain, and you don't pour a skyscraper onto wet sand just because the sand has a nicer brochure.

That night, with rain tapping softly against the windowpane and a dozen C++ headers open across his screen, he closed the glossy pitch decks for good. He didn't need anyone's permission to trust his own eyes. The code had already spoken.

Frank's Memory Note

Slide decks sell dreams, but source code tells the truth—and a real builder always trusts the metal they can inspect.
