Chapter 13: The OS/2 Warp Experiment

In the middle of the nineties, every serious computer magazine and late-night BBS forum seemed to hold a secret priesthood devoted to three blue letters: IBM OS/2. Its followers spoke about it with the hushed reverence of people who had glimpsed a cleaner future. They called it "a better DOS than DOS and a better Windows than Windows." They boasted about true preemptive multitasking, rock-solid memory protection, and a graphical desktop called the Workplace Shell that made Microsoft's Program Manager look like a cardboard toy.

Frank had a simple rule that would follow him for the rest of his life: never trust hype, and never dismiss it either—install it yourself and watch what the metal does. So he cleared a dedicated partition on his hard drive, sat down with a towering stack of 3.5-inch floppy disks for OS/2 Warp 3.0, and began feeding them into the A: drive one by one. Disk 1. Disk 2. Disk 3. The floppy drive clacked and buzzed in steady mechanical rhythm for over an hour, a test of sheer devotion before a single pixel of the new world appeared.

When the Workplace Shell finally bloomed onto his CRT, Frank leaned closer. It felt genuinely alien—and genuinely brilliant. Everything on the desktop was an object you could inspect, drag, and drop onto a shredder or a printer. Better yet, he put the hype to an immediate stress test: he started formatting a floppy disk while simultaneously running a DOS prompt and dragging a window across the screen. On DOS and Windows 3.1, formatting a floppy froze the entire computer like a deer in headlights. On OS/2 Warp, the mouse cursor glided across the glass like butter on a warm skillet while the floppy drive chugged away undisturbed in the background.

For a few exhilarating days, Frank thought he had found the promised land. One misbehaving program couldn't drag the whole system down with it. The High Performance File System (HPFS) felt sharper and cleaner than FAT16, without the suffocating eight-dot-three filename prison. He even invited a skeptical classmate over just to watch two heavy programs run side by side without stuttering, grinning like a stage magician revealing a real trick.

Then reality crept in through the side door. Half of his exotic hardware—the budget scanner, the latest video card modes, the newest games arriving on CD-ROM—either had half-baked OS/2 drivers or none at all. Getting sound and graphics to cooperate meant hunting through obscure FTP mirrors and CompuServe archives late into the night, patching configuration files by hand while the rest of the world marched blindly toward Microsoft's massive software tide. OS/2 was a fortress, yes, but a fortress with very few roads leading to its gates.

When Frank eventually reclaimed the partition, he didn't feel like he had wasted a single minute. The OS/2 experiment handed him two priceless truths at once. Technically, it proved that smooth, crash-proof multitasking wasn't impossible on humble 486 hardware—meaning any sluggishness he saw in other systems was a software failure, not a hardware excuse. Culturally, it taught him a harder lesson: the best architecture in the room still loses if builders and users can't easily live inside it.

Frank's Memory Note

Installing OS/2 Warp from a mountain of floppies proved that true, silky multitasking was already possible on modest hardware—and that great engineering still starves without an ecosystem.
