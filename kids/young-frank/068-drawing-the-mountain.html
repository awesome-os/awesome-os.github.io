Chapter 68: Drawing the Mountain

The next morning, Frank did something radical for a lifelong computer builder: he turned the monitors off. No terminals, no browsers, no GitHub tabs screaming for attention. He cleared the dining table, laid down a wide sheet of blank white paper, and picked up a simple black ink pen.

Her question from the night before was still ringing like a bell in his mind. If every floor of the house is crooked, why keep sanding the roof? To answer it honestly, he needed to see the whole building at once—not one slice at a time through a tiny terminal window, but the entire structure from bare silicon to the pixels on a user's screen.

He started at the bottom of the page and drew a solid horizontal line: Hardware & Silicon—CPUs, memory buses, GPUs, network cards. Clean, physical, obedient to the laws of physics. Above that, he drew Operating Systems & Kernels—Linux, Windows, macOS—already splitting into three jagged slabs that refused to agree on file paths, processes, or system calls.

Above the kernels, his pen began to move faster. C Toolchains & System Libraries, fractured between compilers and linkers. Virtual Machines & Containers, built to hide the fractured kernels by packing an entire second operating system inside a box. Language Runtimes—Node, Python, JVM, Go, Rust, WebAssembly—each sitting on its own island, duplicating schedulers, memory managers, and standard libraries. Package Managers & Registries, each inventing its own lockfile format and dependency graph. Build Systems & Bundlers, translating code into other code just so the next layer could understand it. And finally, perched precariously at the snowy peak, Frameworks & Cloud Orchestrators, trying to hold the entire wobbling tower together with YAML and hope.

When he finally lifted the pen, he stepped back and stared at what he had drawn. It didn't look like an architecture. It looked like a jagged, top-heavy mountain made of mismatched boulders balanced on each other's edges.

Every single time he had tried to build truly reproducible software during his open-source odyssey, he had been climbing halfway up that mountain and trying to hammer in a straight nail. Even if his own code at the summit was spotless, a tremor six layers down—a shifted kernel ABI, a changed container runtime, a broken package mirror, a host compiler update—would ripple upward and shatter everything he built.

Tracing the ink lines with his fingertip, Frank saw the fault lines all at once. The problem wasn't that open-source developers weren't smart enough. The problem was that everyone was building on the mountain, and almost nobody was daring to look at the mountain itself.

Frank's Memory Note

You cannot truly understand a trap until you step back from the screen, pick up a pen, and draw the entire machine on one sheet of paper.
