Chapter 28: Ten Layers Down

Growing up as the kid who always saw ten layers deeper came with a quiet kind of double-edged magic. Most people walked through the world interacting with the top layer—the glossy user interface of daily life. They pressed a button and trusted the elevator to arrive; they heard someone say "I'm fine" and kept walking; they accepted the rules of school, schedules, and expectations as if they were laws of gravity rather than agreements somebody had made up decades ago.

Frank could never quite stop at the surface. When he stood in a crowded train station, his mind automatically peeled back the plaster: he saw the dispatch timetables, the copper relays switching tracks down the line, the human fatigue in the conductor's shoulders, and the invisible web of deadlines pushing hundreds of strangers toward the same sliding doors. When he opened a piece of software, he didn't just see menus and buttons; he sensed the shortcuts a tired programmer had taken at four in the morning, the leaky abstractions taped together with hope, and the cleaner, simpler machine waiting underneath to be uncovered.

The same depth spilled into how he saw people. During lunch breaks, he noticed the kid who talked the loudest was usually the one terrified of being left out. He noticed when a teacher's strictness was really just exhaustion in disguise, or when a friend's sudden joke was a shield held up against a hard week at home. Seeing ten layers down meant you could never unsee the cracks—and sometimes, if you weren't careful, it made you feel like a visitor from a slightly different planet, standing in a loud room while everyone else danced to a simpler beat.

For a while, Frank wondered if seeing so deeply was a curse. It made small talk feel strangely hollow, and it made shallow rules almost physically irritating to obey. Once, after he pointed out a flaw in a school computer lab's network setup a little too bluntly, a frustrated administrator snapped at him for being a "know-it-all." Frank walked out burning with embarrassment, wondering why trying to make something better always seemed to rub the wrong way.

It was his quiet artist friend who set him straight one afternoon over lukewarm tea. "You don't have a vision problem, Frank," she said, sketching a quick spiral on a napkin. "You just forget that most people aren't standing on the tenth floor with you. If you shout down from ten layers deep, it sounds like an earthquake. If you build a staircase so they can walk down with you, it feels like a gift."

That sentence rearranged something permanent inside him. Depth without warmth was just sharp glass; depth paired with patience became a lantern. From that day on, Frank practiced translating what he saw—not to prove he was the smartest kid in the room, but to make things gentler, clearer, and truer for the people around him.

Frank's Memory Note

Seeing ten layers deeper is only lonely until you learn how to build staircases instead of walls.
