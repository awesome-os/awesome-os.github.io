CHAPTER 04 OF 12 · 1941–1945

Konrad Zuse, the ENIAC Six & John von Neumann

The First Real Electronic Computers
What They Invented

First programmable binary computer (Z3), first electronic general computer (ENIAC), and stored-program architecture

In 1941 in Berlin, Konrad Zuse built the Z3 — the world’s first working, programmable, fully automatic binary digital computer, built from telephone relays in his parents’ living room.

In 1945 in the United States, ENIAC became the first giant all-electronic general-purpose computer. Six brilliant women — Betty Holberton, Jean Bartik, Kay McNulty, Marlyn Wescoff, Ruth Lichterman, and Frances Spence — figured out how to wire and program its 18,000 vacuum tubes with zero manuals!

At the same time, John von Neumann wrote down the "stored-program" blueprint: keep both data and instructions in the same memory. That made computers flexible — though decades later, copying huge data back and forth across one wire became known as the von Neumann bottleneck.

Why It Still Runs Inside Your Device Today

Almost every PC still uses the stored-program design — and modern engineering (like Awesome-OS) works hard to solve the von Neumann copying bottleneck.

Honest History Note

For decades, history books showed photos of the six ENIAC women standing next to the machine and mistakenly called them "models." They were the actual programmers who made ENIAC work!
